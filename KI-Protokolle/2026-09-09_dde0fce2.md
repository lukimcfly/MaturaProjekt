Two real bugs, and one that just makes it sound bad.
1. The mixer node is DC-connected to the base. This is the big one. Since the wiper of each pot has a DC path to ground through the pot's lower half, your three pots sit in parallel with the 10k bias resistor. At mid-travel that's 5k per channel, 1.67k for all three, in parallel with 10k → about 1.4k. Your base voltage becomes
12 V × 1.4k / (47k + 1.4k) ≈ 0.35 V
which is under V_BE, so the transistor is essentially cut off. Worse, the value changes as you move the knobs: pots fully up gives ~0.6 V, fully down shorts the base to ground and kills the stage completely. That's the cursed behaviour — the volume knobs are also bias knobs.
Fix: put a coupling cap (10 µF, + toward the base) between the mixer node and the base.
2. The three wipers are tied directly together. Passive summing with no isolation means each channel's wiper is loaded by the other two, so the pots interact: turning one channel up pulls the others down, and none of them are independent. Put a series resistor (10k–47k) between each wiper and the summing node. You lose some level, but the amp has plenty of gain. The proper answer is an op-amp virtual-earth summer, which gives you real channel independence and unity control per input — worth doing if you have a spare op-amp, since you're already on op-amps for the square oscillator.
3. The gain is absurd, so anything that survives the above will clip. With the 100 Ω emitter resistor fully bypassed by 470 µF, r_e ≈ 26 mV / I_E ≈ 2 Ω, so A_v ≈ 330 / 2 ≈ 170. Split the emitter resistor: 10 Ω unbypassed in series with 90 Ω bypassed. Gain drops to ~28 and the distortion goes with it. The flat-topped, notched traces on your scope are this plus problem 1.
Two smaller things: 330 nF into 10k puts your high-pass corner at ~48 Hz, which is right on top of your 60 Hz test tone — you're getting amplitude loss and phase shift before the signal even reaches the mixer. Use 1 µF or larger. And the base input impedance of a bypassed common-emitter stage is only a couple of kΩ and varies with signal level, which loads a passive mixer non-linearly; the summing resistors help, but an emitter follower or op-amp buffer between mixer and amp is the clean solution.
Fix 1 first on its own and see how much of the weirdness disappears — I'd guess most of it.

**Electrically, lowering the source from 5 V to 1 V and turning the pot down to 20 % are the same thing.** Both just reduce how many millivolts arrive at the base. The stage doesn't know which one you did.

What you've ended up with is actually a good place to be. Looking at the sim right now: channels 1 and 3 are at full (`50 / 10k`), channel 2 is at zero (`10k / 50`), and the output is 1.74 V peak, clean. So **knob fully up = maximum clean output**. That's exactly how a mixer channel should be scaled — you want the top of the knob's travel to be the edge of clipping, not somewhere in the middle where 80 % of the rotation is useless.

Two things to keep in mind:

**Your real oscillators won't be 1 V.** In the simulator you can just type a number. On the breadboard, whatever your square/saw/sine circuits put out is fixed — and if the square oscillator is an op-amp on ±12 V rails, that's ~20 V p-p, twenty times what you're simulating. The attenuation has to live somewhere in the circuit. So: measure what each oscillator actually produces, then size the summing resistor per channel to bring it down. A hot channel might need 100 k where a quiet one needs 22 k.

**All three channels up will be ~50 % louder than what you're seeing.** Right now only two are contributing. Turn on the sawtooth and check it still doesn't clip before you call the levels final.

**And the clipping was one-sided.** Look back at where it flattened — it was the top of the wave hitting the rail, not both ends.
